New knobs and pulls are the cheapest change that makes a kitchen look different. Budget packs run about $2–$4 per piece, mid-range pieces roughly $5–$15, and designer pulls $15–$30 or much more. Hiring someone to install them adds about $5–$15 per piece in one 2026 guide and up to $30 in another. For a kitchen with about 32 openings (our assumed example below), that works out to roughly $65–$130 in budget hardware if you install it yourself, and about $320–$800 for mid-range hardware installed by a pro.

The number that matters most is not the price tag on the hardware. It is whether the new pulls match the screw holes already in your doors and drawers. If they do, the job takes a screwdriver. If they do not, you are filling and redrilling, which costs time, risks visible patches, and is a problem if you rent.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Hardware per piece:** about $2–$4 for contractor packs, $5–$15 for mid-range, $15–$30 and up for designer or solid-metal pieces.
- **Pro installation:** about $5–$15 per piece in one February 2026 guide, $5–$30 in Bob Vila's guide. Handymen may instead bill $50–$100 per hour.
- **Typical kitchen (our arithmetic):** 32 pieces costs about $64–$960 in materials depending on tier, and about $224–$1,440 if a pro installs.
- **Check the hole spacing first.** Common center-to-center sizes are 3 in (76 mm), 3-3/4 in (96 mm) and 5 in (128 mm). A match means no drilling at all.
- **Renters:** keep every original knob and screw, and only buy pulls that fit the existing holes.
</Callout>

## Price per piece by tier

Sources agree on the broad shape: plain knobs are very cheap, and the price climbs with material and brand. Bob Vila lists plastic at $1–$5, wood at $1–$10, stainless steel at $4–$32, and brass or bronze at $5–$200 per piece, with custom or antique handles starting around $500 each (that article was last modified in January 2024). Retail listings on a design site we read give more concrete examples, though the page does not state a date.

| Tier | Typical price per piece | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | about $2–$4 | Contractor packs of 10 or 25 in plain finishes |
| Mid-range | about $5–$15 | Knobs from major hardware brands at $4.50–$10 and pulls at roughly $8–$17 in some of the listings we saw (the same site also lists pulls above and below that) |
| Premium | about $15–$30, higher for long pulls | Matte black or brass designer pulls; one 12-inch drawer pull was listed at $72 |

The tier boundaries are our grouping of those prices, not an industry standard. Size matters too: appliance pulls and long pulls for pantry doors cost more, and installers also charge more for them.

## Matching your existing holes

This is where cheap upgrades turn into expensive ones. Pulls attach with two screws, and the spacing between the centers of those two holes is what retailers call center-to-center, or CTC. It is not the length of the pull.

| Common CTC | Metric | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3 in | 76 mm | Listed as a popular size |
| 3-3/4 in | 96 mm | Listed as a popular size |
| 5 in (5-1/16) | 128 mm | Listed as a popular size |
| 6-5/16 in | 160 mm | Longer pulls for wide drawers |

Those sizes come from the standard drill-center list on a hardware retailer's sizing page. Single-hole knobs fit any knob or a T-bar pull, so a knob swap never has a spacing problem.

How to measure it: remove the old pull and measure from the center of one screw hole to the center of the other. Measuring an installed pull gives you overall length, which is the wrong number. A hardware-retrofit guide recommends millimeters, because 96 mm and 3-3/4 in (95.25 mm) are close but different enough to stop screws from starting, and a 128 mm hole pair will fight a 5 in (127 mm) pull. Check every opening type, not just one: drawers, pantry doors and replaced doors may not share a spacing.

If the spacing does not match, you have four options:

1. **Pick a pull that does.** Many pulls are made in dual-spacing versions that fit two common sizes.
2. **Use a backplate.** A plate covers the old holes, and the guide says to size it to the full spread of the old holes and order screws about a quarter inch longer.
3. **Fill and redrill.** Use hard or two-part wood filler on painted wood, sand it flush, then prime and touch up. On stained solid wood the same guide suggests a dowel of the same species, and warns the patch will still show up close. Thermofoil and laminate do not take filler, so a mismatch there means a backplate or new doors.
4. **Buy a knob instead.** One hole is enough, but note that a single centered drawer knob hole cannot be reused as one end of a pull without throwing the pull off-center.

## DIY or hire a pro

A straight swap into matching holes is a beginner job. One renter-focused guide calls a swap into matching holes a 30-minute upgrade, mentions a 20-opening kitchen, and warns to use a manual screwdriver rather than a power drill to avoid stripping the door material. That time estimate is optimistic if you are removing old hardware you have to keep track of, but the job is still measured in an hour or two for most kitchens. The CountBricks case study recorded about 4.6 hours of professional labor for 60 pieces, which is roughly 4.6 minutes per piece.

Drilling new holes is different. The retrofit guide recommends a jig with adjustable stops so every front gets the same pattern, a sharp brad-point bit, and a sacrificial backer clamped behind the door to prevent blowout. Hand-marking many doors makes small errors visible across a whole run of cabinets. A painter's tape template, which Sandiego Hardware suggests, lets you test positions before you drill.

Choose DIY if:

- Your new hardware matches the existing holes or uses a backplate.
- You are fine with a few hours and a screwdriver.
- You own the cabinets, or the landlord has approved changes.

Choose a pro if:

- You are drilling new holes in 20 or more doors and want identical placement.
- The doors are expensive, solid wood, or already refinished, so a slip is costly.
- Your count is large and your time is worth more than $5–$15 per piece.

Placement conventions help either way. On slab doors one guide puts the hole about 2 inches in from the bottom and side edges, and on panel doors it suggests aligning knobs and pulls with the rail. For drawers, a pull about a third of the drawer's length is a common rule, and wider drawers often take two pulls.

## Renting? Swap safely

Replacing hardware is one of the more renter-friendly upgrades because it is reversible, as long as you do not drill. Check your lease first, since drilling into landlord-owned cabinets violates many leases. One guide calls mismatched spacing that forces new holes a major lease violation that is hard to hide.

A renter-safe routine looks like this:

1. Remove one pull and bring it to the store, or measure CTC in millimeters before ordering.
2. Buy only pulls that fit the existing spacing, or knobs on single-hole doors.
3. Put the old hardware and its screws in a labeled, sealed bag and store it somewhere you will remember. Missing or stripped originals can lead to charges for a full replacement set.
4. At move-out, reinstall the originals and take the new hardware with you.

If you cannot match the holes without drilling, ask your landlord in writing, or skip this upgrade. Deposit rules vary by state, and this is not legal advice.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring overall length instead of CTC.** The pull is longer than the spacing between its screws.
- **Assuming every opening matches.** Drawers and doors may use different spacings, so record each type before ordering.
- **Rounding between inches and millimeters.** The 96 mm and 3-3/4 in difference is under a millimeter but can still stop screws from starting.
- **Buying a short pull for a wide drawer.** Use a longer pull or two pulls.
- **Forgetting screw length.** Thicker drawer fronts, doubled fronts and backplates need longer screws. One guide mentions 1.75-inch breakaway screws for doubled-up drawer fronts.
- **Filling holes and expecting an invisible fix.** Stained wood, thermofoil and satin finishes show patches.
- **Throwing away the originals.** This is the most costly mistake for renters.
- **Not testing placement.** Tape marks on the doors take two minutes and avoid drilled mistakes.

## Ways to save

- **Buy a multi-pack.** Contractor packs of 10 or 25 are the cheapest per piece in the sources we read.
- **Match the holes you have.** Skipping new drilling saves both the labor and the repair risk.
- **Mix prices, not looks.** Spend more on the pieces you touch every day and less on rarely used cabinets, using the same finish so they look consistent.
- **Order a few spares.** One guide recommends about 5% extra when standardizing hardware across many openings.
- **Spend on hardware before a bigger project.** If your cabinets are worn out and not just dated, see our comparison of [refacing, repainting and replacing](/blog/cabinet-refacing-vs-repainting-vs-replacing) and the [kitchen cabinet painting cost guide](/blog/cost-to-paint-kitchen-cabinets). ## Cabinet hardware FAQ

### How much does it cost to replace all the knobs and pulls in a kitchen?
For about 32 openings, our arithmetic gives roughly $64–$128 for budget hardware, $160–$320 for mid-range and $480–$960 for premium, if you install it yourself. A pro adds about $160–$480 in labor at $5–$15 per piece. Published whole-kitchen totals for pro installation range from about $120 to $2,400, depending on the hardware.

### Can I put new pulls on old holes?
Yes, if the center-to-center spacing matches. Remove an old pull, measure from the center of one screw hole to the center of the other, and buy that size. Common sizes are 3 in (76 mm), 3-3/4 in (96 mm) and 5 in (128 mm). Dual-spacing pulls fit two sizes, and backplates cover old holes if the spacing differs.

### How do I fill old cabinet handle holes?
On painted wood, use hard or two-part wood filler, sand it flush, then prime and repaint. On stained solid wood, a same-species dowel with matched grain gives a better result but may still show up close. Filler does not bond well to thermofoil or laminate, so use a backplate or replace the door.

### How many knobs or pulls does a kitchen need?
One per door and drawer is the usual starting point, with two pulls on wide drawers. One source says a 200 sq ft kitchen needs 20 to 40 pieces, and another case study counted 60. Count your own doors and drawers rather than relying on an average.

### Is it cheaper to install cabinet hardware myself?
Yes. Labor is about $5–$15 per piece in one 2026 guide and up to $30 in another, so DIY saves roughly $160–$480 on 32 pieces at those rates. It also avoids a handyman's hourly minimum. The savings shrink if you botch drilled holes, so hire help when you need new holes in many doors.

### Can renters change cabinet knobs and pulls?
Often, as long as nothing is drilled and your lease allows it. Keep the original hardware and screws in a labeled bag and reinstall them before you move out. Ask your landlord in writing if you are unsure, and remember that rules vary by state.
