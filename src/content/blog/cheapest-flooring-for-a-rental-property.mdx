The cheapest floor to buy is not always the cheapest floor to own. In cost guides published in 2026, sheet vinyl comes out at about $1 to $4 per square foot installed, laminate about $3 to $7, budget carpet about $3 to $8 and luxury vinyl plank (LVP) about $4 to $9. Tile and engineered hardwood start near $7 and $8. For a landlord, the number that matters is closer to cost per year of life, and carpet loses that comparison in most of the sources we read.

This guide compares eight options for a 600 sq ft rental unit, shows where the sources disagree, and works out a cost-per-year table. We did not test any product, and every cost-per-year figure is our own arithmetic from the ranges cited. If you are a tenant covering a floor temporarily, see our [renter-friendly flooring comparison](/blog/renter-friendly-flooring-cost-comparison) instead.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Lowest upfront cost:** sheet vinyl at about $1 to $4 per sq ft installed, or $600 to $2,400 for 600 sq ft.
- **Best-supported all-rounder:** LVP at about $4 to $9 installed in the 2026 pages we read, with lifespans quoted from 15 to 25 years (10 to 15 in an active rental, per one landlord guide).
- **Shortest life:** carpet, quoted at 4 to 6 years in a rental guide and 5 to 15 in a budget-flooring guide.
- **Per year of life (our arithmetic):** sheet vinyl about $75 to $150, LVP about $156 to $260, budget carpet about $220 to $660 for a 600 sq ft unit.
- **Hidden costs:** removing the old floor adds about $1.50 to $3.50 per sq ft in one guide, and leveling or subfloor repair can cost more on top.
</Callout>

## Cost per year of life: how landlords compare floors

A floor that costs twice as much but lasts four times as long is the cheaper floor. One landlord guide makes the same point with an example: carpet at $6 per sq ft replaced every 5 years against LVP at $8 per sq ft lasting 15 years. Applied to 600 sq ft, that is about $720 a year for carpet and about $320 a year for LVP (our arithmetic).

The table below uses one source for both price and lifespan so the numbers are comparable: a budget-flooring guide published in July 2026. We take the midpoint of its installed price range, multiply by 600 sq ft, and divide by the low and high ends of its lifespan range.

| Option | Installed midpoint | 600 sq ft at midpoint | Lifespan in the source | Cost per year (our arithmetic) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sheet vinyl | $2.50 | $1,500 | 10–20 years | $75–$150 |
| Laminate | $5.00 | $3,000 | 10–25 years | $120–$300 |
| Budget carpet | $5.50 | $3,300 | 5–15 years | $220–$660 |
| LVP / SPC | $6.50 | $3,900 | 15–25 years | $156–$260 |
| Porcelain / ceramic tile | $11.00 | $6,600 | 25–50+ years | $132–$264 |
| Engineered hardwood* | $13.00 | $7,800 | 20–40 years | $195–$390 |

*That guide gives no installed price for engineered hardwood, so we used the midpoint of a separate July 2026 range of $8 to $18. Treat that row as the least precise.

Lifespans on retailer and guide pages tend to be generous, and rentals are hard on floors. The January 2025 landlord guide puts LVP at 10 to 15 years in an active rental and carpet at 4 to 6 years. On those shorter lifespans the same $3,900 LVP floor costs about $260 to $390 a year, and the $3,300 carpet floor about $550 to $825 a year. Sheet vinyl still wins on cost per year, tile is competitive if it survives, and carpet stays the most expensive to own in both versions.

## Durability, water and repairs

- **Luxury vinyl plank.** Waterproof surface, and a damaged plank can be replaced without lifting the whole floor, according to the landlord guides we read. One guide recommends a wear layer of at least 20 mil for rentals, and says 6 to 12 mil products are meant for light residential use. It also says WPC cores are softer than SPC. Another source warns that waterproof describes the plank, not the whole floor, because water can still travel through seams to the subfloor. Our [vinyl plank installation cost guide](/blog/cost-to-install-vinyl-plank-flooring) goes deeper on thickness and pricing.
- **Sheet vinyl.** Cheapest and very water resistant, but seams are the weak spot and sharp objects can tear it.
- **Laminate.** Cheap and decent against scratches, but the wood-based core swells when wet. Sources say to keep it out of kitchens, bathrooms and anywhere below grade.
- **Carpet and carpet tiles.** Carpet absorbs stains and odors, and a burn or deep stain on broadloom usually means replacing the floor. Carpet tiles can be swapped one at a time, and a guide dated August 2025 puts their life at 10 to 15 years in moderate use.
- **Tile.** The most durable and water resistant option in the sources, but hard and cold underfoot, and cracks over a flexing subfloor. Unsealed grout stains and admits water.
- **Hardwood.** Looks premium but scratches, and is vulnerable to spills and pet accidents. Solid wood can be refinished many times, and a guide says engineered wood can be refinished about 4 to 6 times.
- **Peel-and-stick vinyl.** One July 2026 guide gives it a 2 to 5 year life because adhesive fails and edges curl. It is cheap per square foot and a poor buy for a long hold.

## Hidden costs that change the answer

The per-square-foot figures above are for installing the new floor. These are priced separately and can swing the total.

| Extra cost | Range in the sources | Our math on 600 sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| Removing old flooring | $1.50–$3.50 per sq ft (Angi, via a BuildDirect guide, Aug. 2026); $1–$6 (This Old House, June 2026) | $900–$2,100 at the Angi range |
| Carpet removal | Up to about $1 per sq ft (Inch Calculator) | Up to about $600 |
| Tile demolition | $3–$7 per sq ft (Rubi, 2026) | $1,800–$4,200 |
| Leveling the subfloor | $1–$5 per sq ft (Inch Calculator, vinyl); $2–$5 (Rubi) | $600–$3,000 if the whole floor needs it |
| Subfloor replacement | $3–$10 per sq ft (Angi) | $1,800–$6,000 |
| Underlayment | $3.40–$5.30 per sq ft, materials and install (This Old House) | Mostly a hardwood and tile item; some planks come with it attached |
| Backer board or uncoupling membrane for tile | $4.00–$5.20 per sq ft (America's Floor Source, Mar. 2026) | $2,400–$3,120 |

Trim, baseboards and transitions are also extra labor in several guides, though none gave a figure we could use.

**Waste factor.** Most guides say to buy about 10% extra for cuts. That turns 600 sq ft into 660 sq ft of material. Tile with complex or diagonal layouts can need 15% to 20% (Rubi), and a simple engineered hardwood room 5% to 10% (Factory Flooring Liquidators). Buying a few extra planks from the same dye lot also gives you spares for future repairs.

<Callout type="warning" title="Moisture and subfloor first">
Several guides say leveling, a moisture barrier and a clean, flat surface can decide whether a warranty stays valid. Fix a damp or flexing subfloor before you spend on any floor, because a rushed install that has to be redone costs more than doing it right once.
</Callout>

## Turnover, pets and noise

**Turnover.** The January 2025 landlord guide says LVP can usually be cleaned and re-rented within one to two days, while carpet may need professional cleaning or full replacement and add several days of vacancy. Our [move-out cleaning guide](/blog/move-out-cleaning-diy-vs-professional) covers what that cleaning costs.

**Pets and kids.** A May 2026 This Old House guide on dogs ranks porcelain tile as the most durable choice and calls laminate the worst because the core swells with moisture and the hollow feel amplifies nail clicks. It says urine can seep into vinyl seams and weaken glued-down installs, so sheet vinyl suits high-risk areas. It also lists carpet as a poor fit, with carpet tiles as the easier option. The guide covers dogs only and none of our sources address kids. Our inference is that heavy wear is mostly a moisture and stain question, which favors tile, sheet vinyl and plank over carpet or laminate.

**Noise in multi-unit buildings.** Carpet with pad is quieter. A consultant's guide (updated August 2026) puts carpet with pad on a concrete slab at IIC 65 to 75 and floating LVT at about IIC 50 to 56, rising to 56 to 60 with acoustic underlayment. It also says the International Building Code sets an IIC 50 lab baseline between dwelling units and that many jurisdictions enforce it. That is a consultant's claim, not a code citation we verified, so check your local code, your lease and any association rules before you swap carpet for a hard floor in an upper unit.

## What to choose by situation

| Your situation | Pick this | Why, per the sources | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quick turnover, tight budget | Sheet vinyl, or LVP with a thick wear layer | Lowest cost per year; fast clean and re-rent | Seam tears in sheet vinyl; thin wear layers |
| Mixed units, one floor throughout | LVP or SPC | Waterproof surface, single-plank repairs | Subfloor flatness; buy spare planks |
| Bathrooms and kitchens | Porcelain tile or sheet vinyl | Best water resistance | Tile labor, grout sealing, backer board |
| Pets or heavy wear | Porcelain tile, sheet vinyl or LVP | Handles accidents better than laminate or carpet | Seams that admit liquid |
| Upper unit with noise rules | Carpet tiles, or hard floor plus acoustic underlayment | Carpet with pad has far higher IIC ratings | Local code and building rules |
| Bedroom where tenants expect carpet | Carpet tiles | Replace single tiles instead of the room | Lifespan of 10 to 15 years at best |
| Premium unit, longer hold | Engineered hardwood | Refinishable, 20 to 40 year lifespan in one guide | Highest installed cost; water damage |

## Depreciation: a short note

Cost per year is a budgeting tool, not a tax figure. IRS Publication 527 (2025) lists flooring and wall-to-wall carpeting among examples of improvements, which are generally capitalized instead of deducted as repairs, and says carpeting in a residential rental falls in the 5-year property class. It sets 27.5 years for residential rental buildings. How a specific hard-surface floor is classified is a question for a tax professional. This is not tax advice.

## Tenant damage and deposits

A durable floor still gets damaged. Whether you can charge for it depends on your state's deposit rules and your lease, and many states distinguish normal wear from damage. Our guide to [getting your full security deposit back](/blog/get-your-full-security-deposit-back) shows the tenant-side checklist, and it is worth photographing the floor at move-in and move-out. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying on price per square foot alone.** The cost-per-year table above shows how the order flips.
- **Choosing a thin wear layer.** A cheap plank that wears through in a few years is the carpet problem again.
- **Skipping subfloor prep.** Uneven floors telegraph through plank, and damp ones ruin laminate.
- **Using laminate in wet rooms.** Sources are consistent on this.
- **Mixing floor types room by room with awkward transitions.** One continuous floor is cheaper to buy and to repair.
- **Forgetting spares.** Keep leftover planks or carpet tiles from the same batch.

## Flooring for rentals FAQ

### What is the cheapest flooring for a rental property?

Sheet vinyl is the cheapest installed option in the guides we read, at about $1 to $4 per square foot, or roughly $600 to $2,400 for 600 sq ft. Laminate and budget carpet follow at about $3 to $8. Stained concrete can also be cheap if a good slab is already there. Cheapest upfront does not always mean cheapest per year.

### Is luxury vinyl plank worth it for a rental?

For most units, yes, based on the sources. LVP installs at about $4 to $9 per square foot in 2026 guides, resists water, and lets you replace one plank. One landlord guide puts its life at 10 to 15 years in an active rental. Choose a wear layer of at least 20 mil and verify the product and subfloor requirements before buying.

### How long does carpet last in a rental?

One landlord guide says 4 to 6 years, while a 2026 budget-flooring guide says 5 to 15 years for budget carpet. The gap probably reflects rentals being harder on floors than owner-occupied homes, which is our inference, not a claim from either source. Carpet tiles are quoted at 10 to 15 years in moderate use.

### Is laminate or vinyl better for rentals?

Vinyl is better wherever moisture is possible. Laminate is cheaper in some guides but its wood-based core swells when wet, and sources advise keeping it out of kitchens and bathrooms. Laminate can suit a dry bedroom or living room. For a whole-unit floor, vinyl avoids having two products in one unit.

### Can I deduct new flooring on my taxes as a landlord?

Not necessarily as a repair. IRS Publication 527 (2025) lists flooring among examples of improvements, which are generally capitalized and depreciated, and puts carpeting in a rental in the 5-year class. Rules and classifications are specific, so ask a tax professional. This is not tax advice.
