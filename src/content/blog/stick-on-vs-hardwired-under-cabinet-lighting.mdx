Under-cabinet lighting can cost as little as $15 for a battery kit or $800 and up for a hardwired job, and most of that gap is labor, not lights. Current retail listings put plug-in kits at roughly $20–$60 for a basic strip or bar set, while 2025–2026 cost guides put a hardwired installation at about $250–$800 for a kitchen.

This guide compares battery, plug-in and hardwired lights on price, brightness, running cost, cords and cabinet damage. Published installed prices disagree widely, so we show the spread and mark every calculation that is ours.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Battery or rechargeable:** roughly $15–$32 per set of pucks. Fine for closets and accents, weak for prep work.
- **Plug-in strips and bars:** about $20–$60 for a basic kit, $58–$180 for smart kits.
- **Hardwired:** fixtures around $34–$140 each, plus labor. Whole-kitchen guides say $250–$800; one older guide implies far more.
- **Brightness:** one guide suggests at least 250 lumens per foot. Cheap kits can fall well short.
- **Renters:** choose plug-in or battery. Hardwiring needs a licensed electrician and landlord approval.
</Callout>

## What a professional installation adds

Hardwired lights connect to house wiring, so labor is the big cost. The guides differ.

| Source | Date | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| Rowabi cost guide | Dec 2025 | About $200 labor per fixture on average; typical $200–$800 project, median $400 |
| Vietshop Design | Nov 2025 | Hardwired bars $250–$600 for a kitchen; 2–5 hours at $50–$100 per hour |
| Bees Lighting | 2026 | Hardwired labor $250–$800+; plug-in system $50–$250 |
| Homewyse | May 2026 | Headline of $531–$673 per fixture installed, though its own itemized lines are much smaller |
| Inch Calculator | Sept 2023 | $230–$300 per light installed, 6–8 fixtures per kitchen, $1,500–$2,500 typical |

Read the last two rows with care. Homewyse lists the fixture, basic labor and supplies at roughly $27–$64 each per fixture, which does not add up to its own headline, and we could not reconcile the two. The 2023 guide's per-light price times its six to eight fixtures gives about $1,380–$2,400 (our arithmetic), several times the 2025 range. None of these sources explains the gap. Our inference: per-fixture quotes stack up when a job needs many separate lights, while whole-kitchen figures assume a few longer bars. Ask for a written quote that says how many fixtures it covers.

Per-foot prices are even less consistent. One guide quotes $20–$40 per foot for LED strips, while another prices a 20-foot peel-and-stick strip at $25–$40 in total. Treat any "per foot" number with suspicion until you know whether it means a retail strip or an installed fixture.

## How much lighting does a counter need?

Family Handyman says counters commonly run 8 to 10 feet, as short as 6 in compact kitchens and up to 12 in large ones. Another guide says most kitchens need 8 to 15 feet of lighting across all cabinets. Measure the underside of your wall cabinets, where the lights go, not the counter below.

Here is our own worked example for a 10-foot run at 250 lumens per foot (This Old House's suggestion), or about 2,500 lumens. Lumen figures are listing claims we could not verify.

| Option | What it takes to cover 10 ft | Our estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Battery pucks at 55 lumens each | About 45 pucks | Not practical; use them for spots |
| Wobane 13-ft strip, 1,100 lumens | One kit, about 85 lumens per foot | $20–$26, but roughly a third of the target |
| Litever six-bar kit, 2,000 lumens | Two kits, about 333 lumens per foot | About $108 |
| Hardwired 30-inch bars | Four bars at $34–$114 each | $136–$456 for fixtures |
| Hardwired labor | 2–5 hours at $50–$100 per hour | $100–$500 |

That puts a hardwired 10-foot run at roughly $240–$960 (our sum), which overlaps the $250–$800 the guides give for whole jobs.

## Brightness and color

For task lighting, a June 2026 This Old House guide says at least 250 lumens per foot should give ample light. Lumaz, quoting the Illuminating Engineering Society, gives a range of 200–500 lumens per foot and cites Lighting Research Center targets of 30–50 footcandles on the counter. A third guide says 600–1,000 lumens "at the counter" without giving a length, so it is hard to compare.

On color, two guides recommend 3000K, or 3000K to 4000K, and both warn that 2700K looks yellow and 5000K looks clinical. Most hardwired bars in the Bees listing offer selectable color temperature and dimming. NestAlpha says to mount lights toward the front of the cabinet, behind the lip if there is one, so the counter is lit rather than just the backsplash.

## Battery life and running cost

Battery kits are the one option where the cost keeps coming back. Two guides say a set of standard batteries lasts about 100 hours, or 45 days to two months at two hours a day. Rechargeable lights are quoted at 6 to 12 hours per charge. Lumaz cautions that there is no universal runtime, since LED count, brightness, sensors and battery age all change it.

Our estimate: at three hours a day, 100 hours is about 33 days, or roughly 11 battery sets a year. If a set costs about $4 (our assumption, not a sourced price), that is around $44 a year, more than many plug-in kits cost. A rechargeable light at 6–12 hours per charge needs a top-up every two to four days at that use. Family Handyman's April 2026 test called one rechargeable puck's battery life short and its motion sensor finicky.

Mains-powered lights are cheap to run. Lumaz says a 30-watt LED strip setup can supply 3,000 lumens, and that light bar setups use about 40–60 watts in total. At the June 2026 US average residential rate of 18.34 cents per kWh (EIA), three hours a day works out to about $6 a year for 30 watts and $12 for 60 watts (our arithmetic). That is small enough that running cost should not decide the choice.

## Switches, dimmers, motion sensors and cords

Hardwired lights can be tied to a wall switch, dimmer or smart switch. Plug-in lights use a switch on the fixture, a remote, or a smart plug, which Bees says is less integrated. Rowabi prices adding a wall switch at $100–$200 and Vietshop puts a smart hub and dimmer upgrade near $100, so switch control is a separate line on an electrician's quote. Some plug-in kits include it: Family Handyman's test found a remote on the Wobane kit and button, motion and voice control on the BLACK+DECKER kit.

Plug-in strips and bars need a power adapter and an outlet. Bees says a plug-in system permanently uses one outlet, and Lumaz notes adapters can be hidden in an outlet inside a cabinet. Guides suggest adhesive cable clips and running cords behind the cabinet lip. One also mentions drilling through cabinet frames, which is not a renter move. Bees warns that cutting the plug off a non-rated fixture voids its safety listing and can void home insurance.

## Safety, code and when to hire an electrician

Several guides say hardwiring should be done by a licensed electrician, and one, Ampora (March 2026), says it needs a permit while plug-in lights typically do not. It also says plug-in lights used on countertop outlets fall under the National Electrical Code's GFCI rule. These are commercial blogs, not code text, and rules vary by state and city, including who may do electrical work in their own home. Check with your local building department. This is not legal advice.

The practical rule: if a light connects to house wiring, hire a licensed electrician rather than doing it yourself. If you plug it in, stay within the adapter's rating.

## Renters: adhesive, paint and your deposit

Plug-in and battery lights can come off with the lease. Most use adhesive backing or mounting clips, so choose adhesive or magnetic mounts and skip screws. Wired4Signs identifies forceful, fast peeling as the main cause of paint damage. It suggests lifting an edge gently, warming the strip with a hair dryer on low, peeling slowly, wiping residue with warm water or rubbing alcohol after testing a hidden spot, then touching up any scuffs.

That guide does not address cheap or freshly applied paint, so our caution is an inference: be extra gentle on recently painted cabinets, laminate or thin veneer. Hardwired lighting is a permanent electrical change, so get written landlord approval first. ## Which one should you choose?

| Your situation | Best fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Renting, cook often | Plug-in bars with a hidden adapter | Brighter than pucks, removable |
| Renting, no outlet nearby (pantry, closet) | Rechargeable pucks or bars | No cord, magnetic or adhesive mount |
| Own, tight budget, outlet nearby | Plug-in bars or strips | About $20–$110 for a 10-ft run (our estimate) |
| Own, kitchen remodel with open walls | Hardwired | Cleanest look, no outlet used |
| Own, no remodel, want a wall switch | Hardwired quote vs. plug-in with smart plug | Compare a $250–$800 quote with a smart-plug setup |

Common mistakes: buying by length and ignoring lumens, and pricing only the fixtures for a hardwired job. To save money, watch for sales (the Wobane kit's listed price moved by $6 between two 2026 guides) and light only the prep area, which is our suggestion rather than a sourced tip.

## Under-cabinet lighting FAQ

### How much does under-cabinet lighting cost for a whole kitchen?
For a 10-foot run, plug-in kits come to about $20–$110 in 2026 listings (our estimate), and hardwired jobs $250–$800 across the recent guides. One 2023 guide implies $1,500–$2,500 for six to eight separately installed lights, so ask for an itemized quote that lists the fixture count.

### Can renters install under-cabinet lights?
Yes, if they choose plug-in or battery lights that mount with adhesive, clips or magnets. Hardwiring changes the electrical system, so it needs the landlord's written approval, and sources say a licensed electrician should do it. Check your lease before adding anything permanent.

### How long do battery under-cabinet lights last?
Two guides say a set of standard batteries lasts about 100 hours, roughly 45 days to two months at two hours a day. Rechargeable lights are quoted at 6 to 12 hours per charge. Actual runtime varies with brightness, sensors and battery age.

### Do I need an electrician for under-cabinet lighting?
For hardwired fixtures, the sources we read say yes, and one says a permit is required. Plug-in lights do not need one. Rules vary by state and city, including who may do electrical work in a home, so check with your local building department first.

### How bright should under-cabinet lights be?
One guide gives 250 lumens per foot as a good minimum for task lighting, and another cites 200–500 lumens per foot from the Illuminating Engineering Society. Two guides suggest white light between 3000K and 4000K. Larger kitchens or dark countertops may call for the upper end of the range.

### Will stick-on lights damage cabinet paint?
They can if pulled off quickly. One guide says forceful peeling is the main cause of damage. Warm the adhesive with a hair dryer on low, peel slowly, clean residue after testing a hidden spot, and touch up any scuffs. Be extra gentle on fresh paint or thin veneer.
