In most of the US, a landlord can take money from your deposit for three things: unpaid rent, damage you caused beyond normal wear and tear, and cleaning needed to bring the unit back to the condition you received it in. Ordinary aging, such as faded paint or carpet worn down by foot traffic, is off limits. The catch is that "normal" is not defined the same way everywhere, and your state sets the deadlines.

This guide sums up what official and legal sources say about deductions, how charges are worked out and which deadlines apply in a few named states, then gives you a documentation routine and a move-out checklist. Where sources disagree or cover only one state, we say so.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Wear and tear is not deductible.** Sources separate ordinary aging (faded paint, worn carpet, small nail holes) from damage (burns, large holes, broken fixtures).
- **Deadlines are short and vary.** In the states we checked: 14 days (New York), 21 days (California), 30 days (Texas, Washington). Florida is 15 days with no claim, 30 days to send a notice of claim.
- **Photos decide most disputes.** Dated move-in and move-out photos are your best evidence.
- **You can push back.** Send a written demand letter, then consider small claims court (California's limit for individuals is $12,500).
</Callout>

## What a security deposit is, and how much can be charged

A security deposit is money a landlord holds in case of unpaid rent or damage to the unit. The California Courts self-help guide describes it as usually one to two months' rent, but the actual cap is set by state law and varies. Two examples from official texts:

- **California:** the statute sets the standard limit at one month's rent, on top of first month's rent. Small landlords (individuals or single-member LLCs owning at most two properties with four units in total) may charge two months.
- **New York:** the Attorney General's guide says no more than one month's rent. In buildings with six or more apartments, the deposit must go in an interest-bearing New York bank account.

Elsewhere, check your state attorney general's or housing agency's page.

## Normal wear and tear vs. damage

Wear and tear is the gradual deterioration of ordinary living. Damage is something beyond that, caused by accident, neglect or misuse. The California statute forbids deductions for ordinary wear and tear and for defects that existed before you moved in, but it does not define the term, so "normal" is left to landlords, tenants and, in a dispute, a judge.

The examples below come from FindLaw and two other guides (a tenant-deposit resource site and a California property manager). They agree closely, but they are examples, not a legal test.

| Area | Usually normal wear and tear | Usually damage |
|---|---|---|
| Walls | Small nail holes from pictures, minor scuffs, fading, hairline cracks | Large holes, marker or crayon marks, deep gouges, paint colors applied without permission |
| Paint | Fading from sunlight, general aging | Paint you applied that must be removed or redone |
| Carpet | Matting in traffic areas, light fading, worn spots | Burns, permanent stains, pet urine damage, rips |
| Windows and doors | Dents from door handles, sticky locks, worn weather stripping | Broken glass or screens, damaged locks or doors |
| Kitchen and bath | Worn caulk, minor discoloration, dirty blinds | Heavy grease and grime on a stove, cracked tiles, water damage, excessive mold |

FindLaw's wording on nail holes is "non-excessive," which is the key: a few small picture holes are ordinary, a wall full of anchor holes may not be. Our comparison of [command hooks vs. nails and screws](/blog/command-hooks-vs-nails-screws) covers what each option leaves behind.

## How deductions are usually calculated

FindLaw says a deduction should reasonably match the effort or expense involved, and that cleaning should restore the unit rather than improve it. If you hand the place back as clean as you received it, you should not owe extra cleaning fees.

Some sources go further and describe prorating by useful life: a carpet or paint job has a limited lifespan, so a tenant who damages an old one pays only for the life it had left. The figures we found are California-focused:

- One California property-manager guide says courts treat interior paint as having a useful life of about two years and carpet as seven to ten years. Another gives carpet as eight to ten years, from about five for lower-grade carpet to ten or more for high-quality carpet.
- That second guide's worked example: if a carpet with a ten-year life is damaged after five years, the landlord could typically deduct about half the replacement cost.
- A general tenant guide gives paint a typical life of two to five years, so the range is wide.

A further guide says plainly that there is no universal legal carpet lifespan and that rules vary by state. Treat these numbers as California-flavored, not a national rule. Our own hypothetical illustration: a carpet worth $1,000 new, ruined after using up 80% of a ten-year life, would leave roughly $200 chargeable under strict proration.

Paperwork matters too. In California, deductions totaling more than $125 must come with bills, invoices or receipts, and for in-house work, a description of the task, time spent and hourly rate. Washington's statute also requires copies of estimates or invoices. A bare list of round numbers is worth questioning, and our [bedroom painting cost guide](/blog/cost-to-paint-a-bedroom) can help you sanity-check a repaint charge.

## Deadlines for returning your deposit

Deadlines, the trigger that starts the clock and the required action all differ by state. Here is what the official pages and statutes we read say:

| State | Deadline as the source states it | Consequences the source mentions |
|---|---|---|
| California | 21 calendar days after you vacate: return the deposit or send an itemized statement with receipts | Court guide: if the landlord acted in bad faith, two times the deposit in damages on top of the deposit |
| New York | 14 days after you move out (for units that are not rent regulated): return the deposit or send an itemized "receipt" | Landlord must return the entire deposit; up to twice the deposit if the violation is deliberate |
| Florida | 15 days to return the deposit if there is no claim; 30 days to give written notice of a claim by certified mail | The tenant has 15 days after receiving a claim notice to object in writing |
| Texas | On or before the 30th day after you surrender the premises | Not covered on the page we read; check the statute's penalty sections |
| Washington | 30 days after the tenancy ends and the unit is vacated, by delivering or mailing a full and specific statement | Landlord is liable for the full deposit and cannot assert claims to keep it; up to double for intentional refusal |

Some sites that compile all 50 states list different numbers from the statutes above for the same state, so treat your state's official text as final. Our own practical suggestion: give your landlord your forwarding address in writing, with the date, before you leave.

## Document the unit at move-in

Without a record of the unit's condition when you arrived, you cannot easily show that a scuff or stain was already there.

  caption="Photograph every room and surface on day one, including floors, appliances and the inside of cabinets."
/>

Washington's statute says the landlord must give you a written checklist of the condition and cleanliness of the unit, fixtures, appliances and furnishings at the start of the tenancy, signed and dated by both of you, with a copy for you. If a deposit is collected without that checklist, the tenant may recover the full deposit plus court costs and attorney fees.

California now writes photos into law: landlords must photograph the unit after move-out starting April 1, 2025, and take move-in photos starting July 1, 2025. Even where no such rule exists, the same evidence helps you.

What to capture:

- **Every room and surface,** with date-stamped photos or a walkthrough video: floors, walls, ceilings, appliances, tub grout, window screens and closets.
- **Close-ups of existing damage,** such as carpet stains, chipped tile and wall holes, with a coin or tape measure for scale.
- **Your notes on the landlord's checklist,** where you disagree. Email photos and notes soon after move-in so there is a timestamped record.
- **Age of items,** if you can find out. One guide advises asking for installation or purchase dates and invoices for claimed work.

## Move-out checklist

California and New York both let tenants ask for an inspection before leaving. In California the landlord must tell you about this right in writing after you give notice, and you can request it no earlier than two weeks before the tenancy ends. New York's guide says the landlord must let you attend and identify repairs or cleaning needed, so you can fix them first.

This checklist is our own arrangement of steps the sources describe or imply, not an official form.

| When | Task | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| At notice | Reread your lease; ask in writing for a pre-move-out inspection if your state offers one | Lets you fix listed issues before they become deductions |
| Two to three weeks out | Compare move-in photos with the current condition | Shows what is wear and what could be argued as damage |
| Before moving out | Patch small holes only if the lease or landlord allows; do not repaint in a new color | FindLaw lists tenant-applied paint as damage |
| Last day | Clean everything, including stove interior, fridge, bathrooms, blinds and floors | Excess grime on a stove is an example of deductible cleaning |
| Once empty | Photograph and video every room, date-stamped | Creates your end-of-tenancy record |
| Key return | Send your forwarding address in writing | Gives the landlord an address for the statement and refund |

## What landlords generally cannot charge for

Based on the statutes and guides we read:

- **Ordinary wear and tear.** Every source says this is off limits, including the California and Washington statutes.
- **Defects that existed before you moved in.** The California statute names these directly, which is why move-in photos matter.
- **Cleaning beyond restoring the unit.** FindLaw says cleaning should return the unit to its original condition, not improve it.
- **Charges with nothing behind them.** Where receipts are required, as in California above $125, an unsupported total is open to challenge.

Other questions, such as late fees, pet charges and how much of a carpet's replacement cost you can be billed, are not settled by the sources we read and depend on your state and lease.

## How to dispute a deduction

If the statement arrives and you disagree, take these steps in order.

1. **Read it against the rules.** Check the date, the itemization, any required receipts and the deadline.
2. **Compare it to your evidence.** Match each charge against your photos and emails.
3. **Write a demand letter.** The California guide describes a letter asking the landlord to return the deposit. Our suggestion: state the amount you claim and why, list your evidence, set a reasonable deadline, and keep a copy. One tenant guide advises sending it within 10 to 14 days, but that is advice, not a legal deadline.
4. **Consider small claims.** The California courts say individuals can use small claims for claims under $12,500; other states have their own limits. Washington's statute allows up to double the deposit for intentional refusal to return it, and New York's Attorney General says up to twice the deposit for a deliberate violation.

For state-specific help, start with your attorney general's tenant page or your state court's self-help site.

<Callout type="note" title="Not legal advice">
This is general information, not legal advice. Security deposit rules vary by state, and sometimes by city or type of housing, and they change. Check your state's current statute or an official tenant resource, or talk to a tenant organization or attorney, before acting on a dispute.
</Callout>

## Security deposit FAQ

### Can a landlord keep my deposit for nail holes?
It depends on how many and how big. FindLaw treats non-excessive nail holes as normal wear and tear, and another guide lists small picture-hanging holes as ordinary. Large or numerous holes are more likely to count as damage. Check your lease and state rules before patching or painting.

### What if my landlord misses the deadline?
It depends on the state. New York's Attorney General says a landlord who misses the 14-day itemized receipt deadline must return the whole deposit. Washington's statute says the landlord cannot assert claims to keep it. Read your own state's rule.

### Do I pay full replacement cost for a damaged carpet?
Not necessarily. Several California-focused guides prorate the charge by the carpet's remaining useful life, while another says there is no universal lifespan and rules vary. Ask for the carpet's age and the invoice.

### Should I repaint before I move out?
Not unless your lease or landlord asks. FindLaw lists tenant-applied paint that must be removed as deductible damage. If your lease requires a specific color at move-out, follow it exactly.
