A typical bedroom of 12 by 14 feet with 8-foot ceilings needs about 2 gallons of wall paint for two coats by the math, so you buy 3 gallons or 2 gallons plus a quart. A small 10x10 room needs 2 gallons, and a large 16x20 room with 9-foot ceilings needs about 4. Those figures use the standard formula: wall area, minus openings, times coats, divided by the coverage on the can.

The coverage number is where guides disagree. Most put a gallon at 350 to 400 square feet per coat, while a few manufacturer pages allow as little as 250 on rough surfaces. This page shows the result at both ends, then covers primer, ceilings, rounding, quarts versus gallons and the mistakes that leave you with an empty can on the last wall. All room examples are our own arithmetic, not a quote.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Formula:** perimeter x ceiling height, minus about 20 sq ft per door and 15 per window, times coats, divided by 350-400 sq ft per gallon, rounded up.
- **Coats:** plan on two for walls and one for ceilings.
- **Coverage:** a gallon covers about 350-400 sq ft per coat on smooth, primed walls. A quart covers about 100 sq ft. Texture, bare drywall and big color changes cut that.
- **Rounding:** always round up to whole containers, and keep about 10% extra or a spare quart for touch-ups.
- **Primer:** about 200-300 sq ft per gallon, so a gallon of primer covers less than a gallon of paint.
</Callout>

## The formula, step by step

Every guide we read uses the same four steps. Only the small constants differ.

1. **Wall area.** Add the lengths of all walls (the perimeter), then multiply by ceiling height. A 12x14 room has a 52-foot perimeter, so 52 x 8 = 416 sq ft.
2. **Subtract openings.** Guides use about 20 sq ft per door and 15 per window (Dummies, Almanac uses 21 and 15). This Old House subtracts 20 per door and 10 per window, and Bob Vila measures the actual glass and frame. Skip trim in these measurements.
3. **Multiply by coats.** Two coats doubles the area.
4. **Divide by coverage and round up.** Use the figure printed on your can if it has one; otherwise 350 is the safer default.

The coverage number moves the answer far more than the opening constants do: 732 sq ft of paint takes 1.83 gallons at 400 sq ft per gallon but 2.09 at 350, the difference between a spare quart and running short.

Sources differ, so here is the spread we found:

If a label says 400 and your walls are smooth and already painted, 400 is realistic. If the walls are textured, unpainted drywall or going from dark to light, assume the low end. Hunker (updated 2019) suggests adding about 10 percent for textured walls or pre-painted wood, because labels assume smooth surfaces.

## Ceilings, trim and doors

Ceilings and trim are calculated separately because they often take different paint.

- **Ceiling:** length x width, divided by the coverage. Rule of thumb is one coat (This Old House). Our small room has 100 sq ft of ceiling, which is about one quart. The medium room has 168 sq ft, so two quarts or a gallon. The large room has 320 sq ft, so about a gallon (0.8 to 0.9 gallons at 400 to 350 sq ft).
- **Baseboards and trim:** length x width, with 6-inch baseboard counted as 0.5 ft wide (Almanac). One example from This Old House puts about 69 sq ft of trim at roughly a quart for one coat.
- **Doors and windows:** Dummies counts about 20 sq ft per door, doubled if you paint both sides, and about 7.5 sq ft per window for sash and trim.

A quart is usually enough for trim in a single room (Almanac and Hunker). Bob Vila notes that two quarts often cost about the same as a gallon, so if you will reuse the color, the gallon makes sense. For ceiling pricing, see our [cost to paint a ceiling](/blog/cost-to-paint-a-ceiling) guide.

## How to round up without overbuying

Paint comes in quarts, gallons and 5-gallon buckets, so the answer is never exactly the math. Three rules cover most cases.

1. **Round to whole containers.** Bob Vila says to round up and buy slightly more than the math suggests.
2. **Use the remainder.** Dummies suggests that if the leftover fraction is under half a gallon, buy a couple of quarts to go with your gallons, and if it is over half, buy another gallon. That fits the medium room: 2.09 gallons is 2 gallons plus a quart, if your can says 350 or better and your walls are smooth.
3. **Add a buffer.** The Almanac says pros buy about 10% more than calculated, and This Old House suggests 1-2 spare quarts for touch-ups, noting custom-mixed colors are hard to match later. Buy custom-mixed paint all at once so every can matches.

## Primer: how much and when

Primer covers less than paint: about 200-300 sq ft per gallon per This Old House and Sherwin-Williams (product labels vary, so check yours). This Old House's primer formula is wall area divided by 300, one coat.

For our medium room, 366 sq ft of wall divided by 300 is 1.2 gallons, so you buy 2 gallons of primer, or 1 gallon plus a quart if the label says 300 or more. Hunker says a separate primer needs one coat, so do not double it, and that self-priming paint can reduce or remove the need.

Prime when you are painting new or bare drywall, patched areas, stains or going from a dark color to a light one. This Old House and Dummies both note that tinting primer close to the topcoat color makes a dramatic change easier, and Hunker adds that it can reduce how much expensive topcoat you need. The Almanac says primer can reduce total paint needed on new drywall or over dark colors.

## Mistakes that waste paint

- **Forgetting the second coat.** Doubling is where most first-time estimates go wrong. A single coat of the medium room is about 1 gallon; two coats is 2.1.
- **Using 400 sq ft on rough walls.** Textured or unpainted surfaces cover less, as low as 250 per Bob Vila, so the number on the can is a best case.
- **Skipping the buffer.** If the last wall runs dry, a new can of mixed color may not match exactly. Buy all custom paint at once.
- **Painting dark-to-light without primer.** You can end up with a third coat at topcoat prices instead of one coat of cheaper primer.
- **Buying a gallon for trim.** Hunker says a quart of trim paint may cover three to six interior rooms, so a gallon of trim paint is often more than you will use.

## What to do with leftover paint

Benjamin Moore says opened paint generally lasts about two years, though temperature swings and environment can shorten that. Its storage advice:

- Keep paint dry, out of direct sunlight and between 60 and 80 degrees F. A garage is acceptable only if the paint does not freeze.
- Put the lid back on right away, clean the rim, lay plastic wrap between lid and can, and tap the lid shut with a rubber mallet.
- If you move paint to a smaller container, pick one not much bigger than the amount left, to limit air.
- A sour smell or chunky, jelly-like texture means the paint is spoiled.

Keep one quart for touch-ups and label it with the brand, color, product, sheen and room; Benjamin Moore also suggests writing this on the back of a light switch plate. For the rest, check your local rules for disposal; they vary by area. Renters should confirm the lease first, since many require walls returned to their original color. ## Paint quantity FAQ

### How many gallons of paint do I need for a 12x12 room?
A 12x12 room with 8-foot ceilings has about 334 sq ft of wall after one door and two windows (our arithmetic). Two coats is 668 sq ft, which is 1.7 gallons at 400 sq ft per gallon or 1.9 at 350. Buy 2 gallons if your walls are smooth. If they are textured, plan on 3 or add a spare quart.

### How much does one gallon of paint cover?
One gallon typically covers 350-400 sq ft per coat, according to Sherwin-Williams and This Old House. Behr lists 250-400 depending on color and surface. Sherwin-Williams suggests a little more than a gallon for painting a 10x10 room. Rough, unpainted or very dark surfaces reduce coverage, so check the number on your specific can.

### Do I need two coats of paint?
Two coats is the rule of thumb for walls, with one for ceilings, according to This Old House. The Almanac says bold or bright colors often need at least two. Double your calculated area unless you have a clear reason not to.

### Should I buy a gallon or four quarts?
Buy the gallon. At MaterialCalc's 2026 quart range of $15-$28, four quarts costs $60-$112, while gallons often run $35-$55. Quarts make sense only for small amounts, such as trim, a ceiling in a small room or touch-ups. Check the can for the coverage and price of your chosen paint.

### How much primer do I need?
Primer covers about 200-300 sq ft per gallon, so divide wall area by 300 for a minimum. A 366 sq ft room needs about 1.2 gallons, so you buy 2. Primer needs only one coat. Self-priming paint can reduce the need, but bare drywall, stains and dark colors usually still call for primer.
