The most valuable things you can do in the first week of a rental cost little or nothing: photograph the unit, test the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, find the shut-offs and ask about the locks. The shopping is the smaller part. Guides put the cost of basic household essentials for a first apartment anywhere from $200 to $1,500, so $200 will not furnish a place, but it can cover the gaps that hurt in week one.

Below: a checklist in the order we would do it, a priced shopping plan that totals $169.99, and a list of what can wait. Prices come from Target listings we read on September 21, 2026. The plan and its totals are our own arithmetic, and we did not test any product.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Free first:** document the condition, test alarms, locate shut-offs, ask whether the locks were rekeyed. Cost: $0, plus an hour or two of your time (our estimate).
- **Recurring cost to plan for:** renters insurance averages about $13 a month in one January 2026 analysis, and $15 to $30 in others.
- **Our $200 plan:** six groups of purchases, $169.99 in total, leaving $30.01 for an alarm if one is missing or expired.
- **Bigger figures:** sources put full first-apartment essentials at $200 to $500 or more, and total move-in cash at 2 to 4 months of rent.
- **Can wait:** decor, throw pillows, big furniture and gadgets until you have lived there a few weeks.

## What moving in costs before you buy anything

Your first $200 competes with much larger bills. One 2026 move-in guide says the real total is usually two to four months of rent, counting the first month, a deposit of one to two months, application fees of $25 to $75 and moving. An August 2026 sample budget for $1,400 rent reaches $6,450 (our sum of its lines), including $500 for household essentials and $200 for utility setup.

Two costs can arrive in week one and are not in our $200 plan. One guide says utility companies may ask for a $100 to $300 deposit if you have no history with them, and a Texas provider says a soft credit check or a credit-reference letter can waive it. Renters insurance comes below. For how your own deposit is protected, see [getting your full security deposit back](/blog/get-your-full-security-deposit-back).

## Week one: the free tasks and the small bills

### Document the unit

Photograph and video every room, including the closets, cabinets, utility spaces and balconies people skip. One renter guide recommends timestamped photos, a narrated video walkthrough, and a written checklist that you and the landlord both sign and date, plus written repair promises, ideally as a lease addendum. Look for cracks, water stains, mold, broken hardware and pest signs.

Our suggestion: run every tap, flush every toilet and plug something into each outlet, then email photos of anything that fails the same day.

### Test the smoke and CO alarms

The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends testing smoke and CO alarms monthly and replacing their batteries yearly. A state fire-safety page gives the placement: smoke alarms in every bedroom, outside each sleeping area and on every level; CO alarms on every level and near sleeping areas. The U.S. Fire Administration adds two details that matter in a used apartment. Smoke alarms need replacing 10 years after the manufacture date, which is printed on the back, and they belong on the ceiling or high on a wall.

So press each test button, then check the date (and photograph it, our suggestion). If an alarm is missing, chirping or expired, tell the landlord in writing. Who must supply it depends on your state and lease. Never pull a battery while cooking, which the USFA says can be life-threatening.

### Find the shut-offs

The main water valve is usually near the meter: indoors in cold climates, sometimes in an underground box in mild ones. Fixture valves under sinks and behind toilets turn clockwise, typically two to four turns, says a home-repair guide. A local TV news guide says to label the breakers in the electrical panel.

Gas is different. Both sources advise against operating gas valves yourself, and Family Handyman says to leave the building and call the utility or fire department if you smell gas. The sources do not address renters. In a multi-unit building the main valves may be outside your unit, so ask the landlord or manager where they are (our suggestion).

### Ask about the locks

Ask in writing whether the locks were rekeyed since the last tenant. Some places require it: an Illinois law covers counties with more than 3 million residents and applies when the new tenant has a written lease, with exemptions for small owner-occupied buildings. One landlord-side guide says leases typically forbid tenant lock changes or require you to give the landlord a key, so changing them yourself can breach the lease. Rules vary by state, and this is not legal advice.

If you do pay, expect this per lock:

| Source | Per-lock rekey price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Yelp cost guide (August 2026) | $35–$165, average $140 | Based on quotes; trip fee averages $75 |
| Homewyse (September 2026) | $101–$237 | Includes two new keys |

The sources differ and do not explain why. Yelp suggests one visit for several locks to avoid repeat trip fees.

### Sort out renters insurance

Your landlord's insurance does not cover your belongings, according to both the National Association of Insurance Commissioners and the New York Department of Financial Services. A renters policy typically covers your property, personal liability and living costs if you cannot stay in the unit after an insured loss.

Prices vary by source. NerdWallet's January 2026 analysis puts the U.S. average at $151 a year, about $13 a month, for $30,000 of property coverage, $100,000 of liability and a $500 deductible; $10,000 of property cover averaged $112 a year and $50,000 averaged $192. A March 2026 move-in guide says $15 to $30 a month, as does a 2020 NAIC page. New York's regulator cites about $300 a year for around $50,000 of cover. The sources do not explain the gaps. Our inference is that limits, deductibles and location drive them, so compare quotes on identical terms. New York's regulator also suggests photographing your belongings before you buy and says smoke detectors and deadbolts can earn discounts.

### How to use it

- **Choose the order, not the brands.** If money runs out, priorities 5 to 7 can wait a week. Priorities 2 to 4 cannot.
- **Buy hooks before reaching for a hammer.** See [Command hooks vs. nails and screws](/blog/command-hooks-vs-nails-screws) and our [renter-friendly upgrades guide](/blog/renter-friendly-upgrades-with-prices) for the deposit tradeoffs.
- **The tool kit is the first cut.** Target's search page said most kits include hammers, screwdrivers, pliers, tape measures and utility knives. Borrowing costs nothing.

## Why $200 is not the whole story

The sources put the essentials bill well above $200. A June 2026 guide lists $50 to $100 for a bathroom setup and $150 to $700 for basic kitchen gear. A February 2026 guide says $500 to $1,500 for basic necessities, depending on location and whether you buy new or used. Settl says $200 to $500 and the Bellhop example uses $500. The pages do not itemize those totals, so we cannot say which is right. Our plan is deliberately narrower: first-week gaps, not a kitchen, couch or bed. Starting from nothing, treat it as step one and spread the rest over a month or two.

## What can wait

- **Decor and throw pillows.** Both a June 2026 guide and a February 2026 guide put them at the back of the line.
- **Full furniture sets.** A moving-company guide says to get a bed, seating and a small table first and add the rest as you learn the room. Measure doorways before ordering anything big, because a sofa that cannot get through the door is money wasted.
- **Kitchen gadgets and small appliances.** One February 2026 guide's first-week kitchen kit is a pan, utensils, a plate, a mug and dish soap.
- **Painting and wall coverings.** These usually need landlord approval, and a DIY bedroom is [about $50 to $160 in paint alone](/blog/cost-to-paint-a-bedroom).

## Common move-in mistakes

1. **Relying on a verbal promise.** Get repair promises in writing; a checklist both of you signed is, in our view, stronger evidence than memory.
2. **Assuming the landlord's policy covers your things.** Two official sources say it does not.
3. **Forgetting the boring items.** One checklist singles out the plunger, trash cans, extension cords and a toilet paper holder, and warns against buying everything at once.
4. **Changing the locks without telling anyone.** Many leases require notice or a key for the landlord.
5. **Leaving alarms untested.** The date and the test button take a minute.

## Move-in FAQ

### How much cash do I need to move into an apartment?

One 2026 guide says usually two to four times monthly rent, once you add the first month, a one-to-two-month deposit, fees, moving and setup. An August 2026 example for $1,400 rent reached $6,450 with a $2,000 cushion. Your lease and moving method change this a lot.

### Do I really need renters insurance?

Your landlord's policy does not cover your belongings, per the NAIC and New York's regulator. NerdWallet puts the U.S. average at $151 a year as of January 2026; other guides say $15 to $30 a month. Check your lease for any requirement and compare quotes with identical limits.

### Can I change the locks on a rental?

Often only with permission. Some places require landlords to rekey between tenants, and many leases restrict tenant changes or require you to give the landlord a key. Ask in writing and check your state's rules. This is not legal advice.

### How often should I test the smoke and CO alarms?

Monthly, according to the CPSC and a state fire-safety page, with batteries replaced at least yearly. The USFA says to replace smoke alarms 10 years after the manufacture date printed on the back.
