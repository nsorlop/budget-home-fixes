For a typical 30-square-foot kitchen backsplash, peel-and-stick tiles cost somewhere between $30 and $450 in materials, depending on which price guide you trust. Real tile installed by a professional lands around $500 to $1,700 in most 2026 guides, and doing the tile yourself can cost $335 to $900 once you buy the tools. Peel-and-stick is cheaper and faster, but it is a different product with different limits, especially near a stove.

This guide puts the published numbers side by side, flags where sources disagree, and gives decision rules for renters and owners. Where a figure is our own arithmetic rather than something a source states, we say so.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Peel-and-stick materials:** guides quote $1 to $15 per square foot, so about $30–$450 for 30 sq ft (our calculation). No special tools needed.
- **Professional tile:** about $480–$1,500 for 20–30 sq ft and $500–$1,700 for 30–40 sq ft in two 2026 guides. A third puts 20–40 sq ft at $900–$3,500.
- **DIY tile:** about $335–$900 for 30 sq ft of ceramic if you buy the tools (our calculation). Tools eat much of the labor saving.
- **Behind the stove:** manufacturers that publish limits say 120–140°F and 6–8 inches of clearance from burners. Sources disagree on whether peel-and-stick belongs there at all.
- **Renters:** peel-and-stick is the only realistic option to undo. Tile is permanent.
</Callout>

## How big is a "typical" backsplash?

Guides disagree, which partly explains their differing totals. Block Renovation (December 2025) says 20 to 40 square feet, Artistic Stone (updated July 2026) says 20 to 30, Property Blueprint (2026) says 30 to 40, and Yelp (September 2026) prices a 16 sq ft job.

Measure your own: length of wall covered times height. A 12-foot run of counter tiled 18 inches up is 12 x 1.5 = 18 sq ft (our example, not a standard). Property Blueprint notes that going full height, counter to cabinet, can double or triple the area compared with a 4 to 6 inch strip. We use 30 sq ft as a round working number below.

For a real listing, Art3d's stone-look pack is $29.99 for 9.5 sq ft on its own site, about $3.16 per square foot (our division).

### DIY tile: what you buy

Doing it yourself removes the labor line, which Block Renovation puts at $500 to $1,200 on a standard 30 sq ft job, but adds a tool bill. Block itemizes a wet saw at $150–$400, a nipper and spacers at about $50, and thinset plus grout at $75–$150. Add ceramic at $2–$10 per sq ft (about $60–$300 for 30 sq ft, our multiplication) and you reach the $335–$900 figure above. Defusco Industrial Supply also lists a notched trowel, grout float, level and safety gear (no sourced prices found), and Defusco and Erin Zubot Design both say a saw can be rented, without giving a rental price. Peel-and-stick needs only a tape measure and a knife or scissors, per Family Handyman and Rubi.

Buy extra tile: Property Blueprint says 10% to 15%, Erin Zubot says 20%. Buying every tool leaves little saving at the low end of the pro range (our arithmetic on ranges from different guides, so a rough check only). The saving is real if you borrow tools or tile a large area. Block also warns that first-timers often end up with uneven grout lines, tiles set proud of the wall and sloppy cuts around outlets.

## Install time

Stickwoll, a peel-and-stick seller, says two to four hours with nothing to dry, and Redfin says a weekend. For tile, Stickwoll says one to two days, Erin Zubot describes a weekend with grouting the day after tiling, and Artistic Stone says professionals typically take one to three days. Plan for the long end on a first attempt.

## Heat and grease behind the stove

This is the sharpest split in the research. Family Handyman says peel-and-stick is not suitable near stoves, and Kitchen Bath Geeks says to avoid it right behind the stove because repeated heat cycles hurt the adhesive. Bob Vila (June 2025) says most, but not all, peel-and-stick backsplashes are heat resistant and tells you to read the product literature.

The manufacturers that publish numbers set firm limits:

- **Smart Tiles** says its tiles can go behind a stove, but leave an 8-inch clear zone if the stove has no rear control panel or metal plate, keep surfaces at or under 120°F, avoid direct heat and open flame, and stay 2 inches from countertop appliances.
- **Aspect** (peel-and-stick glass and metal) says the tile tolerates 140°F, its adhesive should stay at or under 150°F, and burners should be at least 6 inches away.
- **Art3d** says its stone-look tiles resist stovetop heat, but its product page gives no temperature or distance.

None of the sources we read says how hot the wall actually gets behind a range, so follow the manufacturer's clearance number rather than a general rule. Our inference: if the strip directly behind the burners can pass 120–140°F while you cook, that strip is better left as it is or done in real tile. Grease matters less. Smart Tiles says to clean with a soft sponge and mild, non-abrasive soap.

## How long each one lasts

| Source | Peel-and-stick | Tile |
|---|---|---|
| Family Handyman | 5–20 years if installed and kept well; wears faster near water or heat | not stated |
| Rubi (tile-tool maker) | 5 to over 20 years | outlasts peel-and-stick |
| Bob Vila | 3–5 years or longer; premium stone or marble up to 10 | not stated |
| Stickwoll (seller) | 3–5 years, up to 10 with care | 50+ years |
| Kitchen Bath Geeks | budget 1–2, premium 3–5; its FAQ says 5–10 | decades |

Our reading: 3 to 10 years is the middle of these claims, depending on the product, prep and how close it sits to heat and water. Where sources compare the two, all say tile lasts far longer.

## Surfaces and prep

Smart Tiles lists painted drywall, smooth ceramic tile, acrylic panels and mirrors, says you can stick over an old smooth ceramic or glass backsplash, and advises against textured walls. Aspect says drywall, plaster and clean glazed ceramic work, porous or textured surfaces do not, and damaged spots need repair and latex primer first. Art3d lists textured walls as compatible. That is a genuine disagreement between manufacturers, so check your product.

Fresh paint is a trap. Smart Tiles says wait 21 days after painting, or 72 hours after primer, before applying. Our [peel-and-stick wallpaper vs. paint guide](/blog/peel-and-stick-wallpaper-vs-paint) covers the same cure-time logic. For tile, Cardinal Decor says the wall should be rigid, flat, clean and dry, and suggests checking with a straightedge for humps and dips.

## Renters: removal and your deposit

Tile is a permanent change, so it needs written approval from your landlord and comes out only by demolition. For peel-and-stick the picture is more mixed than "removes cleanly":

- Smart Tiles says its tiles come off with heat and leftover adhesive wipes away with a cleaner.
- Flooring Clarity rates removal as moderate, suggests warming each tile with a hair dryer for 30 to 60 seconds, and says the risk rises on unsealed drywall and fresh paint.
- Aspect warns that its aggressive adhesive can damage drywall and that repairs may be needed.
- Family Handyman says permanent versions feel like duct tape, so buy one sold as removable.
- The Kitchen Bath Geeks author says tiles took chunks of paint off after 10 months. That is one anecdote, not a rate.

Practical steps: photograph the wall first, ask for permission in writing, use a product marketed as removable, and stick only to smooth, fully cured painted drywall. Our [security deposit guide](/blog/get-your-full-security-deposit-back) covers how wall repairs get itemized, and the [renter-friendly upgrades list](/blog/renter-friendly-upgrades-with-prices) has other reversible options.

## Which should you choose?

- **Renter:** peel-and-stick on a smooth, cured wall, with permission and photos. Skip the burner zone or keep to the clearance in the product's instructions.
- **Owner staying a few years:** peel-and-stick can freshen a dated wall for under $450 on 30 sq ft. If you are listing soon, price simple ceramic or subway tile, which starts around $500 professionally installed. That is our judgment, not a sourced rule.
- **Owner staying long term:** tile. Ceramic or subway keeps costs down, and glass or stone is where a pro earns the higher labor rate. DIY is fine if you can borrow a saw.
- **Old backsplash you do not want to demolish:** Smart Tiles says its tiles can go over smooth old ceramic or glass, which could skip the $3–$6 per sq ft removal. Only if the surface is truly smooth.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the dry layout.** Cardinal Decor and Bob Vila both say to lay it out first.
- **Sticking to fresh paint or textured walls.** Most manufacturers we read call for clean, dry, smooth surfaces.
- **Starting with an unlevel row.** Erin Zubot calls a level first row critical, and Property Blueprint says to set the counter first because the backsplash is measured against it.
- **Copying clearance numbers from another kitchen.** Use your product's distance from a burner.
- **Buying a permanent adhesive when you rent.** Strong-adhesive tiles can pull paint or drywall.

## Backsplash FAQ

### Can I use peel-and-stick behind a stove?
Sources split. Family Handyman says no, Bob Vila says most are fine if the literature says so, and manufacturers that publish numbers give 6 to 8 inches of clearance and a 120–140°F ceiling. If your product lists no heat rating, keep it away from burners.

### How long does peel-and-stick last?
Sources range from 1 to 2 years for budget products to 5 to 20 for good ones on well-prepped walls. About 3 to 10 years is the middle. Heat, moisture and poor prep shorten it.

### Will peel-and-stick damage my walls?
It can. Aspect warns its adhesive may damage drywall, and Flooring Clarity says the risk is higher on unsealed drywall and fresh paint. A smooth, fully cured painted wall and a product labeled removable lower the risk without removing it.

### Is DIY tile cheaper than hiring a pro?
Somewhat. Block Renovation puts the labor saving at $500 to $1,200 on 30 sq ft, but tools and supplies of $275 to $600 come out of that. It pays off if you already have tools or want to learn.
