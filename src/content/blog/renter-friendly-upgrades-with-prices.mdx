Most renter-friendly upgrades cost less than $150 at 2026 retail prices, and the cheapest ones (a tension rod, LED bulbs, a pack of adhesive hooks) come in around $3 to $15. What separates them is not the price tag. It is what happens when you take them down.

This guide covers ten upgrades. Each one has a price range from a retail page we read in September 2026, a deposit-risk rating with the reason behind it, and one thing to check before you buy. The risk ratings are our own judgment, built from the caveats manufacturers and renter-advice sites publish. We did not test any product, and none of these is guaranteed to leave your walls or floors untouched.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Lowest risk:** rugs on the right floor, tension rods and curtains, freestanding shelves, static-cling window film, and bulbs. Most run $3–$150.
- **Low-to-medium and medium risk:** peel-and-stick wallpaper and backsplash. The adhesive can lift paint that is flat, fresh or has had years to bond, so test a patch first.
- **Highest risk on this list:** peel-and-stick floor tiles, because residue on a subfloor is hard to clean. Get permission first.
- **Waiting after painting:** Command says 7 days, Tempaper says 30. When sources differ, use the longer one.
- **Small nail holes** are normal wear and tear in the sources we read. Drilling, painting and wiring are a different category.
</Callout>

## What the deposit rules say

Small nail holes from hanging pictures count as normal wear and tear in the guides we read, so a landlord generally cannot charge for them. Large holes from drilling, clusters of holes and holes that crack the paint around them can be charged. One landlord-side guide, last updated in 2023, says a flat fee of $5 to $25 per hole is typical. A 2026 renter guide puts the "normal" limit at roughly one to three small nail holes per wall.

State law and your lease decide the rest, and this is not legal advice. Our guide to [getting your full security deposit back](/blog/get-your-full-security-deposit-back) covers the common deductions. We found no sourced figure for what removing damaged wallpaper or floor tile typically costs a tenant, so the ratings below are based on how each product can fail, not on a dollar amount.

## The ten upgrades, one by one

### 1. Adhesive hooks and strips

A six-hook pack of Command medium hooks (3 lb per hook) was $8.99 at Target, and a comparable value pack was $12.49 at Staples. Most homes need two to four packs, which gives the $9–$48 range above.

Risk: low. The listing tells you to wait 7 days after painting, avoid wallpaper, clean the spot with rubbing alcohol and pull the strip slowly straight down. The same Target page carried reviews reporting adhesive failure or wall damage, so outcomes vary. Check first that the paint is cured and the hook's weight rating covers what you hang. More in [Command hooks vs. nails and screws](/blog/command-hooks-vs-nails-screws).

### 2. Removable wallpaper

Target's peel-and-stick rolls ran $21.99–$35 and covered about 30.75 sq ft each. An 8 by 10 ft accent wall is 80 sq ft, so three rolls, or $66–$105 (our estimate).

Risk: low to medium. Tempaper's FAQ says flat and matte paint is not recommended because paint can come off with the wallpaper, and it advises waiting at least 30 days after painting. It also says it cannot guarantee a clean removal from existing wallpaper. A blogger who has removed a dozen-plus installs reported one 3–4 inch patch of paint pulled off after three years, and called it her worst case. That is one person's experience, not data. Check the sheen of your paint and stick a test piece somewhere hidden. Compare costs in [removable wallpaper cost per room](/blog/removable-wallpaper-cost-per-room) and [peel-and-stick wallpaper vs. paint](/blog/peel-and-stick-wallpaper-vs-paint).

### 3. Peel-and-stick backsplash

Target sold four-piece packs covering about 2.3–2.6 sq ft for $12.49–$12.89, and branded Smart Tiles packs for $24.99–$32.99. That works out to roughly $5–$13 per sq ft (our calculation), so a 20 sq ft backsplash lands at $96–$260. Bob Vila's cost guide, updated in 2023, gives a wider $4–$30 per sq ft for materials.

Risk: medium. RenterFriendly.com says the adhesive can bond hard over time and can lift paint on painted drywall, and that these tiles work best on clean, smooth, non-porous surfaces such as tile or laminate. Check what your wall is made of and how greasy it gets. See [peel-and-stick backsplash vs. tile](/blog/peel-and-stick-backsplash-vs-tile).

### 4. Peel-and-stick floor tiles

Target listed 20 twelve-inch tiles for $24.99 on sale ($49.99 regular), 50 tiles for $42.50, and 36 wood-look planks for $76.90. For 100 sq ft, that is about $85 at the cheapest to $250 at regular prices (our estimate).

Risk: medium to high. Hunker's removal guide says sticky residue will be left on the subfloor and that older tiles get stickier with time. RenterFriendly.com calls floors the highest-stakes peel-and-stick category and says tiles left up for years raise the risk. Check your lease and get permission before opening the box. Loose-lay options are in our [renter-friendly flooring cost comparison](/blog/renter-friendly-flooring-cost-comparison).

### 5. Area rugs

Target's 5x7 rugs started around $15–$30, with mid-range at $80–$160 and premium at $200–$400.

Risk: low, with one catch. Floor & Decor says rubber- or latex-backed rugs should not go on any vinyl flooring, because they can discolor or stain it. Check your floor type before buying, and pick a backing or pad that says it is safe for that floor.

### 6. Tension rods and curtains

At Target, small tension rods started at $2.97 on clearance, and 60 to 72 inch rods ran $9–$13. Curtain panels were $12–$45 each and pairs $20–$55. One window is roughly $23–$68 (our sum).

Risk: low. A renter-advice blog says tension rods hold by pressure alone and leave no marks. Check the rod's weight rating so heavy blackout panels do not bring it down. For closets, see [over-door organizers vs. closet systems](/blog/over-door-organizers-vs-closet-systems).

### 7. Stick-on and plug-in lighting

Battery stick-on lights ran $6.79–$10.99 for singles and pairs, $19.99 for a three-pack with a remote, and $29.99 for a rechargeable pair of light bars. Plug-in wall sconces started at $21.99, with many at $40–$90. Four-packs of standard LED bulbs were $6.99–$9.99, smart bulbs $10.39–$12.99 and Wi-Fi smart plugs $12.99–$29.99.

Risk: low to medium for stick-on strips. An LED strip retailer's removal guide says the adhesive can pull paint if you peel it off forcefully, and suggests warming it with a hair dryer and peeling slowly. Plug-in and battery lights avoid wiring altogether. Check the surface you will stick to. ### 8. Cabinet knobs (keep the originals)

Target listed single knobs from $4.27 to $21.59, two-packs at $10–$16 and ten-packs at $27–$32. For 10 to 20 pieces, that is $27–$160 (our estimate).

Risk: low if you keep the originals. One renter-advice blog suggests storing them in a labeled bag. Check that the new hardware fits the existing holes. If it needs new ones, that is drilling and belongs in the ask-first list below.

### 9. Freestanding shelving

Target's basic three-shelf unit was $28 and a five-shelf unit $40. Most mid-range bookcases sat between $40 and $150, with higher-end pieces near $280.

Risk: low for the wall, with a catch. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends securing furniture to the wall with anti-tip devices, and the National Council on Aging says to anchor into a stud, which takes under $20 and about 20 minutes. Anchoring means a screw hole, so ask before you do it. Costs are compared in [freestanding vs. built-in shelving](/blog/freestanding-vs-built-in-shelving-cost).

### 10. Removable window film

Target listed static-cling and removable films from $10.19 for a 24 by 36 inch sheet to $29.99 for decorative patterns.

Risk: low for static cling. Gila's FAQ says its static-cling films can be re-applied and adhesive films cannot be re-installed. A recently installed film peels off, while older ones may need a remover kit. Check the manufacturer's instructions for your window type, since Gila says its films go on dual-pane windows only if used correctly.

## Where the sources disagree

- **How long to wait after painting.** Command's hook listing says 7 days. Tempaper says at least 30 for wallpaper, and RenterFriendly.com says 30 for tiles. The sources do not explain the gap. Our inference is that heavier adhesives need more cure time, so use the longer number for anything large.
- **Backsplash prices.** Bob Vila's $4–$30 per sq ft is wider than the $5–$13 we calculated from Target packs. Bob Vila says the top of its range is for high-quality tiles but does not explain how it set the range, and we only sampled one retailer.
- **"Removable" versus "allowed."** Retail listings call these products renter-friendly. A 2026 tenant guide lists adhesive wallpaper among alterations that generally need consent. A removable product is not the same as permission.

## Keep the originals: a checklist

- Photograph every wall, floor and fixture you plan to touch before you start (our suggestion).
- Put swapped cabinet knobs in a labeled bag, one bag per room.
- Keep the original light bulbs and any fixture covers you remove.
- Save the landlord's written reply somewhere you will find it a year from now.
- Set a reminder to reverse each change a few weeks before your move-out date.

## How to ask your landlord

Put the request in writing. A 2026 tenant guide says an email chain is enough and a signed addendum is better. Short and specific gets a faster yes:

> Hi [Name],
>
> I would like to make a few reversible changes in unit [number]: [for example, removable wallpaper on one bedroom wall, adhesive hooks, and new cabinet knobs]. There will be no drilling and no wiring. I will keep the original knobs and put them back before I move out, follow the manufacturers' removal instructions, and repair anything I damage.
>
> Could you confirm by reply that this is fine? If you would rather I skip any item, let me know.
>
> Thanks, [Your name]

<Callout type="warning" title="Get written permission first">
Check your lease, and get permission in writing before you do any of these: painting, drilling or wall anchors beyond a couple of small nail holes, replacing light fixtures or hardwiring lights, installing a ceiling fan, changing locks, and putting up glue-based or adhesive wallpaper. These are the changes a 2026 tenant guide lists as generally needing consent. A landlord can charge you to undo them. Rules vary by state, and this is not legal advice.
</Callout>

## Renter upgrades FAQ

### Can a landlord charge me for small nail holes?

In the sources we read, small nail holes from pictures are normal wear and tear and cannot be deducted. Large holes, clusters of holes and holes that crack the paint can be. State law varies, so check yours and photograph the walls at move-in.

### Do I need permission for peel-and-stick products?

Check your lease. A 2026 tenant guide lists adhesive wallpaper among alterations that generally need consent. Hooks and rugs rarely raise the question, but a short email costs nothing and gives you a record.

### Are stick-on products guaranteed not to damage walls?

No. Tempaper says paint can come off flat or matte walls, and the Command listing we read carried reviews of wall damage. Results depend on the paint, how long the product stays up and how you remove it.
