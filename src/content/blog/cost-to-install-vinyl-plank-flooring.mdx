Vinyl plank flooring costs about $2 to $7 per square foot for the planks themselves in most cost guides, and about $3.50 to $11 per square foot once a professional installs it. On November 2026 retailer listings the planks ran lower: $0.99 per square foot for a thin glue-down plank and $1.99 to $3.49 for click-lock planks with an attached pad. Whether you or a crew lays them decides most of the gap.

This guide compares the cost guides we read with those listings, adds the extras that quotes tend to bury (prep, trim, removal, waste), and works through a 12x15 ft room. We did not test any product or install anything. Every room total is our arithmetic from the sources at the end, and the sources disagree enough that we show the spread instead of one average.

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Materials:** about $2 to $7 per sq ft in cost guides; $0.99 to $3.49 per sq ft on the Floor & Decor listings we read in November 2026.
- **Professional labor:** $3 to $10 per sq ft in two guides, $1.50 to $3 in a third. For a 200 sq ft room, guides put the all-in professional total between about $600 and $4,000.
- **Our 12x15 ft example (180 sq ft):** about $600 to $970 DIY with click-lock planks and trim, or roughly $870 to $2,770 with a pro, before any removal or leveling.
- **Buy 5% to 10% extra,** more for diagonal layouts, and check the subfloor first: manufacturers want it flat, clean and dry.
- **Renting?** Click-lock and loose-lay planks are the options that do not bond to the floor, but their instructions have catches, covered below.
</Callout>

## What a professional install costs

Labor is where sources split. This Old House and Bob Vila give $3 to $10 per square foot (about $36 per hour in both, attributed to Angi and HomeAdvisor). Today's Homeowner gives $1.50 to $3 per square foot in one section and $4 to $12 in another, so that page contradicts itself. None of the three explains its range. This Old House does say labor is higher in urban areas and rises with complicated layouts such as herringbone, and Bob Vila adds tight corners, stairs and appliance moves.

Published totals for materials plus labor, before extras:

We skipped This Old House's 300 sq ft luxury row because it does not scale with the rows around it. Professional installation takes one to two days on This Old House's estimate; Bob Vila says removal plus installation can take a few days.

## DIY vs. professional: a worked 12x15 ft example

A 12x15 ft room is 180 sq ft. Our assumptions: a plain rectangle, straight layout, 10% extra for cuts (one manufacturer's figure), click-lock planks with an attached pad, and an old floor that is flat and can stay. The two products are the 5 mm and 6 mm Floor & Decor listings above. Box sizes are our inference from box price divided by price per square foot (about 30 and 28.6 sq ft), so 7 boxes covers the 198 sq ft we need.

| Line | Low | High |
|---|---|---|
| 7 boxes of click-lock plank | $419 | $699 |
| Trim, transitions, adhesive at $1 to $1.50 per sq ft | $180 | $270 |
| **DIY total** | **$599** | **$969** |
| Professional labor at $1.50 to $3 per sq ft | $270 | $540 |
| Professional labor at $3 to $10 per sq ft | $540 | $1,800 |
| **Professional total (all of the above)** | **$869** | **$2,769** |

Add these only if they apply: removing the old floor ($180 to $360 at $1 to $2 per sq ft), leveling ($180 to $900 at $1 to $5), a separate underlayment ($54 to $108), and tools you do not own. This Old House says tools run "a few hundred dollars" to buy or rent; the Lowe's DIY guide we read lists a pry bar, oscillating saw, utility knife, chalk line, jigsaw with a vinyl blade, mallet, pull bar, spacers and a transition kit.

So DIY saves roughly $270 to $1,800 of labor on this room. We found no source giving DIY hours. Our inference: a first-timer should plan a weekend for 180 sq ft, mostly spent on prep and edge cuts.

## Which type fits: glue-down, click-lock or loose-lay

- **Glue-down** is the thinnest and cheapest plank. This Old House and Inch Calculator both recommend a professional for it, and Today's Homeowner says it needs more expertise than click-lock. Our inference: the adhesive makes it a poor fit for rentals.
- **Click-lock** planks join to each other and float. Today's Homeowner calls them the easiest for DIY, and This Old House groups them with peel-and-stick as the most DIY-friendly. The MSI rigid-core guide says to leave a 3/8 inch gap at walls, and the Lowe's guide says 5/16 inch.
- **Loose-lay** planks grip by weight and friction. Manufacturers' rules are stricter than the marketing, covered in the renter section.

## Wear layer, thickness and water resistance

The wear layer is the clear top coat, measured in mils (thousandths of an inch). MSI's July 2026 post says 12 to 20 mil suits single-family homes, 6 to 8 mil suits low-traffic rooms, and 20 mil is its standard for multifamily and commercial use. Bob Vila recommends at least 12 mil. MSI also says coating quality matters as much as thickness, and that plank thickness does not measure surface durability.

Mind the seller bias. All three Floor & Decor listings carry a lifetime residential warranty, including the 8 mil glue-down plank, so a "lifetime" label does not tell you which plank wears best. The Cape Blanco page lists 20 mil in its specifications but 20 to 28 mil in its description. Check the spec sheet, not the headline.

All three listings call the plank waterproof, and Bob Vila calls rigid core 100% waterproof. Bob Vila adds that pooled water can still seep through and damage the subfloor. Shaw's loose-lay instructions call for silicone at the perimeter in wet areas such as bathrooms, and MSI's guide requires moisture testing on concrete.

## Subfloor, prep and expansion gaps

Prep is what the cheapest quotes skip. What the manufacturers and retailers we read say:

- **Flatness.** Lowe's: within 1/8 inch over 6 feet. MSI, UCX and Shaw (for concrete): 3/16 inch over 10 feet. Shaw wants 1/8 inch over 10 feet on wood. UCX says loose-lay is not meant to bridge irregularities and they may telegraph through.
- **Subfloors.** Lowe's lists concrete, wood at least 1 inch thick, or existing smooth vinyl. Shaw and UCX require existing resilient flooring to be a single layer, non-cushioned and well bonded. MSI says its rigid core can go over wood, non-cushioned vinyl or linoleum, and intact tile (filling wide grout lines), but not carpet.
- **Concrete.** UCX wants slabs at least 90 days old, moisture tested, with a 6-mil poly film between ground and slab. Lowe's calls for polyethylene underlayment on concrete on or below grade.
- **Gaps and trim.** Lowe's allows baseboards to come off or a shoe molding to cover the gap. MSI says to use quarter round if baseboard is hard to remove, and not to fasten trim into the floating floor.
- **Temperature and conditioning.** Shaw wants 65 to 85 F before, during and after. UCX wants 48 hours of acclimation, while MSI says none is needed under proper conditions. Follow your product's sheet.

<Callout type="warning" title="Old vinyl can contain asbestos">
Lowe's says vinyl installed before 1986 may contain asbestos, and This Old House says floors from before the 1980s may need abatement. Shaw and UCX say not to sand, scrape or dry-sweep old resilient flooring. We give no removal steps; contact a professional before disturbing an old floor.
</Callout>

## How much extra plank to buy

UCX tells installers to add 10% for cutting waste. A FlooringClarity guide (May 2025) says 5% to 7% for simple rectangles, 8% to 10% for irregular rooms and 10% to 15% for diagonal layouts, and Inch Calculator says at least 10% for angled layouts. Our 12x15 example uses 10%. Round up to whole boxes and keep a few leftover planks for repairs.

## Renting? What the instructions say

A June 2026 guide from a flooring retailer, D and G Flooring, rates loose-lay as the safest for deposits because nothing touches the subfloor, and says click-lock unclicks and can be reused. The manufacturer instructions we read add catches:

- UCX's loose-lay guidelines call for a 4 inch strip of pressure-sensitive adhesive around the perimeter and at transitions, plus more in a grid in rooms over 12 feet. Shaw says to install tight to walls in rooms under 20 feet, but to add perimeter adhesive in larger areas and full-spread adhesive under rolling loads and chairs with casters. D and G also mentions release tape or removable adhesive dabs in rooms wider than about 10 feet. Check what your product uses and whether the landlord accepts it.
- UCX rules out loose-lay over ceramic tile, hardwood and laminate, and perimeter-glued resilient floors. A rental with those floors may not qualify.
- Click-lock needs a gap at every wall. D and G says renters often hide it with quarter round or removable trim, and MSI says not to fasten trim to the floor.

Our inference: check the lease and get a yes in writing before buying, since the install changes the floor level at doors and trim. This is not legal advice, and state rules vary. ## Which way to go

- **Hire a pro** if you own the home and want glue-down, if the subfloor needs leveling, or if the layout is diagonal or full of corners.
- **DIY click-lock** if the room is a simple rectangle, the subfloor is flat, and you own or can rent the saw and mallet.
- **Loose-lay or click-lock as a renter** only after reading your product's sheet and getting written permission.
- **Stay with a rug** if the floor is sound. The [first $200 move-in budget](/blog/move-in-upgrade-budget-first-200) shows where else the money can go.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing only the planks and forgetting trim, transitions, removal and waste.
- Skipping the flatness check. UCX says subfloor irregularities can telegraph through the plank, and MSI calls a flat subfloor a must.
- Laying planks over carpet or cushioned vinyl, which the instructions above exclude.
- Fastening molding through a floating floor, or closing the wall gap.
- Reading "waterproof" or "lifetime" as proof of durability.
- Disturbing old vinyl without checking for asbestos.

## Ways to save

- Do demolition and trim removal yourself and hire only the laying, if the contractor agrees.
- Buy to your layout's actual waste factor (5% to 7% for a plain rectangle in one guide) rather than rounding up by guesswork.
- Compare listings: the three Floor & Decor planks we read differ by $2.50 per square foot.
- Get at least two written quotes that state whether removal, leveling, trim and transitions are included.
- Reuse existing baseboards where you can. Bob Vila notes reusing old trim means paying only for reinstallation.

## Vinyl plank flooring FAQ

### How much does vinyl plank flooring cost per square foot?
Cost guides put the planks at about $2 to $7 per square foot, and installed at roughly $3.50 to $11. In November 2026 we saw Floor & Decor listings from $0.99 for a thin glue-down plank to $3.49 for a 6 mm click-lock plank with a pad. Luxury and rigid-core lines run higher in the guides.

### Is it cheaper to install vinyl plank myself?
Yes, by the cost of labor, which is $1.50 to $10 per square foot depending on the guide. On our 180 sq ft example that is about $270 to $1,800. Tools, prep mistakes and wasted planks can eat into the saving, and This Old House suggests a professional for glue-down.

### Can vinyl plank go over existing flooring?
Some manufacturers allow it and some do not. MSI says its rigid core can go over wood, non-cushioned vinyl and intact tile, but not carpet. UCX's loose-lay guide allows one layer of non-cushioned resilient flooring, but not tile, hardwood or laminate. Read your product's instructions before buying.

### How much extra vinyl plank should I buy?
Plan on 5% to 10% extra for a simple room. UCX says 10%, and one guide says 10% to 15% for diagonal layouts. Round up to full boxes. The same guide puts irregular rooms at 8% to 10%.

### Is vinyl plank flooring really waterproof?
Sellers label many planks waterproof, and the three listings we read all do. Bob Vila says pooled water can still seep through and damage the subfloor. Manufacturers add conditions: Shaw wants silicone at the perimeter in wet areas, and MSI requires moisture testing on concrete. Wipe up spills promptly.
